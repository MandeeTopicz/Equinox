# Equivalence Logic

## Definition

Two markets from different venues are **equivalent** if they resolve based on the same real-world proposition — meaning a rational observer would expect both to settle the same way (both YES or both NO) based on the same underlying event, within the same resolution window — regardless of differences in title wording, contract structure, fee format, or venue-specific metadata.

This is a definition about the **proposition being resolved**, not surface similarity. "Will the Fed cut rates at the March 2026 FOMC meeting" and "March FOMC: rate cut?" are equivalent. "Will the Fed cut rates in March" and "Will the Fed cut rates by 50bps in March" are **not** — same topic, different resolution criteria, so a rational trader would not expect identical settlement in every scenario. Distinguishing topical relation from true equivalence is where naive text matching fails, and it's the main source of error this methodology has to manage.

## Design principle: precision over recall

A false negative (missing a real equivalence) leaves the system incomplete — a gap to fill later. A false positive (claiming two different markets are the same event) actively produces a wrong routing decision on top of it — a worse failure for infrastructure meant to be trusted. The methodology below is deliberately biased toward under-matching rather than over-matching.

## Methodology: hybrid, in three stages

**Stage 1 — heuristic prefilter.** Cheap, rule-based gates a candidate pair must clear before any similarity scoring runs:

- same category/tag, where venue metadata provides one
- resolution dates within a tolerance window (e.g. ±48h) — markets resolving weeks apart cannot be the same event, regardless of how similar their titles read

**Stage 2 — deterministic equivalence gates.** Discovered necessary, not designed in from the start: prefilter survivors can still share a topic and a date while resolving on fundamentally different criteria (the "50bps" example above), and a high embedding score can't reliably tell the difference on its own — it measures topical closeness, not logical equivalence. Two hard gates run before any scoring; failing either rejects the pair outright, with a concrete, checkable reason, never blended into a score that something else could outweigh:

- **Numeric threshold gate** (plain regex, no AI): extracts magnitude/threshold language — percentages, basis points, dollar amounts, and bare numbers tied to inequality words (above/below/between/etc., since a bare number alone is as likely to be a year as a threshold). If the two titles specify different thresholds, or only one specifies one at all, reject. Same threshold (however phrased) or neither having one: pass.
- **Named-entity gate** (a narrow LLM call — extraction only, never judgment): lists the specific people or organizations named in each title. If the sets differ, reject — "Will Rubio win?" and "Will Vance, Rubio, or Newsom win?" name different entity sets and resolve differently whenever a non-matching name wins, even though they share almost all their wording and resolve on the same date. A title naming nobody specific always passes this gate. See "Why extraction, not judgment" below, and [AI_USAGE.md](AI_USAGE.md).

Both were added directly in response to a real failure found in live data: at the documented `--min-score 0.75` default, a generic same-topic market matched *every* variant of a Kalshi numeric-threshold ladder (21 mutually exclusive "S&P 500 in range X" contracts) independently, and connected-component grouping (see below) chained them into one group — logged as equivalent despite being mutually exclusive by construction. Re-verified directly against that exact case after the gates landed: 0 groups, every pair rejected with its specific threshold mismatch logged.

**Why extraction, not judgment.** The entity gate could instead ask an LLM "are these two markets equivalent?" directly — a single call, no separate deterministic comparison needed. That was considered and set aside: a bare equivalence verdict is a black box a reader can't check against anything, where "list the entities named in this text" is independently verifiable — a human can reread the title and confirm the extracted names are actually there. The equivalence *decision* stays a plain set-comparison rule, fully logged; the LLM only turns free text into structured facts. See [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md) for the fuller tradeoff writeup, including why full LLM-judged equivalence was considered and deferred rather than built.

**Stage 3 — composite similarity score.** For pairs that survive the prefilter and both gates, a weighted score is computed:

| Signal | Weight | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Title/description semantic similarity (OpenAI `text-embedding-3-small` cosine similarity) | 0.60 | Catches paraphrased or reworded titles that pure string matching misses — the main gap rule-based matching alone leaves open |
| Resolution-date alignment (normalized closeness within the tolerance window) | 0.25 | Deterministic, cheap, and a strong disambiguating signal on its own |
| Category/tag match | 0.15 | Weak on its own (categories are coarse) but useful as a tie-breaker |

The composite score lands in `[0, 1]`. Every score is logged with the individual signal values that produced it — the rationale is the breakdown, not just the final number. See [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md) for why OpenAI was chosen as the embedding provider over the alternatives considered.

**Handling missing category data.** The prefilter treats a missing category as "doesn't block" (see above), but scoring is a different question — awarding score for data that doesn't exist would inflate confidence rather than reflect it. When either side of a pair lacks category data, the category weight (0.15) is redistributed proportionally across title similarity and date alignment rather than guessed at either extreme (`composite = (0.60·title + 0.25·date) / 0.85`). In this implementation only Kalshi exposes category data (see [ARCHITECTURE.md](ARCHITECTURE.md)'s venue clients — Polymarket's and Manifold's list endpoints don't expose it without a per-market lookup this prototype skips), so every real candidate pair has at least one side missing it, and the redistributed path is the common case in practice, not an edge case.

**Grouping more than two venues.** A candidate pair scoring at or above the threshold becomes a graph edge; connected components of qualifying edges form match groups, so three markets across three venues that are pairwise-linked (even if not every pair was individually evaluated above threshold) become one group rather than three separate pairs — the point of testing with a third venue at all (see [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md)). A group's reported score and per-signal breakdown are the *minimum* across its qualifying edges, not an average: consistent with the precision-over-recall bias above, a group is only as confident as its weakest verified link.

**Event ids.** `--event` values (e.g. `fed-march-2026-cut`) are generated by slugifying the title of the group's alphabetically-first member (by venue, then venue-native id) — a deterministic, human-legible identifier, disambiguated with a numeric suffix if two groups in the same `match` run would otherwise collide.

## Default threshold: `--min-score 0.75`

This is a reasoned starting point, not an empirically calibrated cutoff — there is no labeled dataset of known-equivalent cross-venue market pairs to validate against in a prototype setting, and that limitation is stated here deliberately rather than hidden. The reasoning: embedding cosine similarity on genuinely paraphrased same-topic text typically scores 0.80–0.95, while merely topically-adjacent-but-different text typically scores 0.50–0.70. `0.75` sits in that gap — closer to the permissive edge than a production system would want, but reasonable for a prototype where matches need to actually surface to be demonstrable. It is exposed as a flag specifically so it can be tuned without a code change, and any run of `equinox match` logs the threshold used alongside its results.

## Known limitations

- No labeled data to validate scoring weights or the threshold against — both are reasoned defaults, not fitted values.
- Two markets can be near-equivalent but structurally different (different thresholds, different sub-conditions) in a way that reads as high title similarity but is not truly equivalent. **This was confirmed at the documented default, not just in the abstract** — once fetch coverage was widened enough to surface Kalshi's numeric-threshold-ladder markets (e.g. 21 mutually exclusive "S&P 500 in range X" contracts), a single generic same-topic market on another venue matched *each* threshold variant independently at `--min-score 0.75`, and connected-component grouping chained all of them into one group. **Fixed** by the deterministic gates described above (Stage 2) — re-verified against the exact same real markets afterward: every one of those pairs is now rejected before scoring, with its specific threshold mismatch logged. The gates catch the two failure patterns actually observed in real data (numeric thresholds, named entities); they are not a general proof of logical equivalence — a structural difference expressed some other way (a different contest type, a different conditional, "by" vs. "at" a date) would still slip through. Extending gate coverage to new patterns as they're found is the expected way this list gets shorter over time, not a one-time fix.
- Only binary (yes/no) markets are matched against each other in this prototype; multi-outcome markets are out of scope.
- In practice, the category signal contributes to almost no real pair's score: only Kalshi exposes category data, so nearly every candidate pair has it missing on at least one side and falls back to the title+date-only redistribution described above. The category weight is exercised by the code and its tests, but rarely by real fetched data.
- Grouping is connected-component (transitive), not clique — a deliberate choice so a genuine 3-way match doesn't require every pair to individually score above threshold (see above). The failure mode this trades in: `--min-score` pushed well below the documented default can chain unrelated markets into one large group through a "hub" market that weakly matches many others (observed at `--min-score 0.5`: several dozens-of-members groups spanning unrelated topics). It's not specific to matching across three venues: the same groups reappear (nearly identical membership) with only two venues configured — a hub market on one venue weakly matching several markets on the other is sufficient on its own; those other-venue markets never even get directly compared to each other (same venue, no candidate pair generated) but still merge transitively through the shared hub.

  **Correction to an earlier version of this doc**: this was originally written to say the chaining failure "doesn't happen at 0.75," based on the threshold repeatedly producing 0 groups in testing. That conclusion held only because the dataset available at the time was too sparse and topically skewed to ever trigger it — not because 0.75 is actually safe from this mechanism. Once fetch coverage improved, the identical transitive-chaining pattern reproduced *at the real 0.75 default*, no threshold games involved (the numeric-threshold-ladder case immediately above is the same underlying mechanism). Leaving this correction here rather than quietly rewriting the original claim, since a "confirmed conservative" statement turning out to be an artifact of the test data, not the method, is itself worth being able to see.
- The named-entity gate's cost is real, not just a per-call rounding error: `extractAllEntities` calls the extractor once per unique candidate market, sequentially, not in parallel or batched into one request (see `internal/match/matcher.go` — a deliberate simplicity-over-latency tradeoff). Verified directly: a targeted `match.Match` call against a handful of markets with no named entities completes in a few seconds, but a full `equinox match` run against the current live-coverage dataset (thousands of prefilter survivors, many naming people) took several minutes and was not run to completion during development. Fine for a prototype's occasional `match` invocation; would need concurrency (a bounded worker pool) before this scales to a much larger candidate set.
