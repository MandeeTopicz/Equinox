# Equivalence Logic

## Definition

Two markets from different venues are **equivalent** if they resolve based on the same real-world proposition — meaning a rational observer would expect both to settle the same way (both YES or both NO) based on the same underlying event, within the same resolution window — regardless of differences in title wording, contract structure, fee format, or venue-specific metadata.

This is a definition about the **proposition being resolved**, not surface similarity. "Will the Fed cut rates at the March 2026 FOMC meeting" and "March FOMC: rate cut?" are equivalent. "Will the Fed cut rates in March" and "Will the Fed cut rates by 50bps in March" are **not** — same topic, different resolution criteria, so a rational trader would not expect identical settlement in every scenario. Distinguishing topical relation from true equivalence is where naive text matching fails, and it's the main source of error this methodology has to manage.

## Design principle: precision over recall

A false negative (missing a real equivalence) leaves the system incomplete — a gap to fill later. A false positive (claiming two different markets are the same event) actively produces a wrong routing decision on top of it — a worse failure for infrastructure meant to be trusted. The methodology below is deliberately biased toward under-matching rather than over-matching.

## Methodology: hybrid, in four stages

**Stage 1 — heuristic prefilter.** Cheap, rule-based gates a candidate pair must clear before any similarity scoring runs:

- same category/tag, where venue metadata provides one
- resolution dates within a tolerance window (e.g. ±48h) — markets resolving weeks apart cannot be the same event, regardless of how similar their titles read

**Stage 2 — numeric threshold gate** (plain regex, no AI): extracts magnitude/threshold language — percentages, basis points, dollar amounts, and bare numbers tied to inequality words (above/below/between/etc., since a bare number alone is as likely to be a year as a threshold). If the two titles specify different thresholds, or only one specifies one at all, reject outright, with a concrete, checkable reason, never blended into a score that something else could outweigh. Same threshold (however phrased) or neither having one: pass. Discovered necessary, not designed in from the start, and added directly in response to a real failure found in live data: at the documented `--min-score 0.75` default (since retired — see "Conjunctive tier floors" below), a generic same-topic market matched *every* variant of a Kalshi numeric-threshold ladder (21 mutually exclusive "S&P 500 in range X" contracts) independently, and connected-component grouping (see below) chained them into one group — logged as equivalent despite being mutually exclusive by construction. Re-verified directly against that exact case after the gate landed: 0 groups, every pair rejected with its specific threshold mismatch logged.

**Stage 3 — composite similarity score.** For every pair that clears stages 1–2, a weighted score is computed:

| Signal | Weight | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Title/description semantic similarity (OpenAI `text-embedding-3-small` cosine similarity) | 0.60 | Catches paraphrased or reworded titles that pure string matching misses — the main gap rule-based matching alone leaves open |
| Resolution-date alignment (normalized closeness within the tolerance window) | 0.25 | Deterministic, cheap, and a strong disambiguating signal on its own |
| Category/tag match | 0.15 | Weak on its own (categories are coarse) but useful as a tie-breaker |

The composite score lands in `[0, 1]` and is still logged with every match decision — it remains useful for ranking pairs *within* a tier and as a reported confidence figure — but it no longer gates anything on its own. Title similarity and date alignment are checked against the conjunctive tier floors (below) immediately, before Stage 4 — a pair that doesn't already clear at least the `needs review` floor here is rejected as `none` without ever reaching the entity gate; see "Conjunctive tier floors" for the floor values and reasoning, and [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md) for why OpenAI was chosen as the embedding provider over the alternatives considered.

**Stage 4 — named-entity gate** (a narrow LLM call — extraction only, never judgment), run *only* for pairs that already cleared Stage 3's `needs review` floor: lists the specific people or organizations named in each title. If the sets differ, reject — "Will Rubio win?" and "Will Vance, Rubio, or Newsom win?" name different entity sets and resolve differently whenever a non-matching name wins, even though they share almost all their wording and resolve on the same date. A title naming nobody specific always passes this gate. Deliberately the last stage, not the second: an entity mismatch can only downgrade a pair that title+date already made look plausible, never promote one that couldn't reach `needs review` on those signals alone, so running it earlier for every prefilter survivor was pure waste — see docs/DECISIONS.md's "Entity extraction: bounded concurrency, retry, and lazy gating" entry for why this reordering was a real reliability fix, not just an optimization (it's what keeps a `match` run's entity-extraction cost bounded by real candidate overlap instead of total fetch-coverage volume). See "Why extraction, not judgment" below, and [AI_USAGE.md](AI_USAGE.md).

**Why extraction, not judgment.** The entity gate could instead ask an LLM "are these two markets equivalent?" directly — a single call, no separate deterministic comparison needed. That was considered and set aside: a bare equivalence verdict is a black box a reader can't check against anything, where "list the entities named in this text" is independently verifiable — a human can reread the title and confirm the extracted names are actually there. The equivalence *decision* stays a plain set-comparison rule, fully logged; the LLM only turns free text into structured facts. See [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md) for the fuller tradeoff writeup, including why full LLM-judged equivalence was considered and deferred rather than built.

**Handling missing category data.** The prefilter treats a missing category as "doesn't block" (see above), but scoring is a different question — awarding score for data that doesn't exist would inflate confidence rather than reflect it. When either side of a pair lacks category data, the category weight (0.15) is redistributed proportionally across title similarity and date alignment rather than guessed at either extreme (`composite = (0.60·title + 0.25·date) / 0.85`). In this implementation only Kalshi exposes category data (see [ARCHITECTURE.md](ARCHITECTURE.md)'s venue clients — Polymarket's and Manifold's list endpoints don't expose it without a per-market lookup this prototype skips), so every real candidate pair has at least one side missing it, and the redistributed path is the common case in practice, not an edge case.

**Grouping more than two venues.** A candidate pair scoring at or above the threshold becomes a graph edge; connected components of qualifying edges form match groups, so three markets across three venues that are pairwise-linked (even if not every pair was individually evaluated above threshold) become one group rather than three separate pairs — the point of testing with a third venue at all (see [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md)). A group's reported score and per-signal breakdown are the *minimum* across its qualifying edges, not an average: consistent with the precision-over-recall bias above, a group is only as confident as its weakest verified link.

**Event ids.** `--event` values (e.g. `fed-march-2026-cut`) are generated by slugifying the title of the group's alphabetically-first member (by venue, then venue-native id) — a deterministic, human-legible identifier, disambiguated with a numeric suffix if two groups in the same `match` run would otherwise collide.

## Conjunctive tier floors

`--min-score` (a single blended cutoff on the composite score above) has been retired. The problem wasn't the specific number — it was the shape: a compensatory weighted sum lets one strong signal offset a weak or contradictory one. Two markets on the same topic, resolving on the same date, but with visibly different titles could still clear a single blended threshold on the strength of date alignment alone (weighted 0.25) even though title similarity — the signal actually carrying most of the semantic-equivalence information (weighted 0.60) — was mediocre. No single cutoff fixes this: raising it rejects genuine paraphrases along with the false positives; lowering it lets more of both through. The failure mode lives in the blending itself, not in where the line is drawn. See [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md) for the fuller writeup of this decision, including the alternatives considered.

The fix: classify each pair into one of three tiers by checking title similarity and date alignment **independently**, each against its own floor — a pair only advances if *both* clear their respective floor for that tier, so a strong score on one axis can no longer paper over a weak score on the other.

| Tier | Title similarity floor | Date alignment floor |
|---|---|---|
| `matched` | ≥ 0.80 | ≥ 0.90 |
| `needs review` | ≥ 0.65 | ≥ 0.70 |
| `none` | below the `needs review` floor on either axis | below the `needs review` floor on either axis |

Both floors in a row must be met for that tier — that's the conjunctive part. A pair with title similarity 0.30 and date alignment 1.00 (an exact date match on an otherwise unrelated title) lands in `none`, not `matched` or even `needs review`, precisely because the old compensatory design would have let the date signal compensate for the near-total absence of title similarity. `internal/match/tiers.go` implements `ClassifyTier`; `internal/match/tiers_test.go` has table-driven cases proving this non-compensatory behavior directly, including that exact scenario.

Category match plays no role in tier classification — deliberately. It's already the weakest signal in the composite score (0.15) and, per "Handling missing category data" below, is missing on at least one side of nearly every real candidate pair, so gating on it would mean gating on absent data more often than present data. The composite score above still folds category in as a reported ranking figure; the tier floors that actually gate routing eligibility use only the two signals present and meaningful for essentially every real pair.

**What each tier means for routing:**

- **`matched`** — clears both floors comfortably. `equinox run`'s auto-select only considers `matched`-tier groups; `equinox route` runs against a `matched`-tier event with no extra flag.
- **`needs review`** — clears the lower floors but not the higher ones: plausibly the same event, not confident enough to route unattended. `equinox route --event <id>` refuses by default with an explanation; `--confirm-review` overrides, explicitly acknowledging the pair hasn't cleared the `matched` threshold rather than silently treating it as if it had. See [ROUTING.md](ROUTING.md).
- **`none`** — doesn't form a match group at all; the pair is simply not linked (see "Grouping more than two venues" above).

The floor values themselves are reasoned defaults, not empirically fitted ones — the same "no labeled dataset to validate against" caveat that applied to the old `0.75` default applies here too (see Known limitations below). What changed is not the availability of ground truth, but the shape of the decision rule: independent floors on the two signals that actually carry equivalence information, instead of one number that any single signal could dominate. Unlike `--min-score`, the floors are fixed code constants (`match.MatchedTitleFloor`, etc.), not a CLI flag or config value — there is no longer a lever to tune per run, which is itself the point: a threshold a user can quietly lower to make more matches "pass" is exactly the mechanism this redesign removes.

## Known limitations

- No labeled data to validate scoring weights or the tier floors against — both are reasoned defaults, not fitted values.
- Two markets can be near-equivalent but structurally different (different thresholds, different sub-conditions) in a way that reads as high title similarity but is not truly equivalent. **This was confirmed at the documented default, not just in the abstract** — once fetch coverage was widened enough to surface Kalshi's numeric-threshold-ladder markets (e.g. 21 mutually exclusive "S&P 500 in range X" contracts), a single generic same-topic market on another venue matched *each* threshold variant independently at `--min-score 0.75`, and connected-component grouping chained all of them into one group. **Fixed** by the numeric threshold gate described above (Stage 2) — re-verified against the exact same real markets afterward: every one of those pairs is now rejected before scoring, with its specific threshold mismatch logged. The threshold gate and the named-entity gate (Stage 4) together catch the two failure patterns actually observed in real data (numeric thresholds, named entities); they are not a general proof of logical equivalence — a structural difference expressed some other way (a different contest type, a different conditional, "by" vs. "at" a date) would still slip through. Extending gate coverage to new patterns as they're found is the expected way this list gets shorter over time, not a one-time fix.
- Only binary (yes/no) markets are matched against each other in this prototype; multi-outcome markets are out of scope.
- In practice, the category signal contributes to almost no real pair's score: only Kalshi exposes category data, so nearly every candidate pair has it missing on at least one side and falls back to the title+date-only redistribution described above. The category weight is exercised by the code and its tests, but rarely by real fetched data.
- Grouping is connected-component (transitive), not clique — a deliberate choice so a genuine 3-way match doesn't require every pair to individually clear a tier (see "Grouping more than two venues" above). The failure mode this trades in: a "hub" market that clears at least the `needs review` floor against several otherwise-unrelated markets chains all of them into one group, even though those other markets are never directly compared to each other (same venue, no candidate pair generated) — they merge transitively through the shared hub. This isn't specific to matching across three venues; the same shape reproduces with only two configured. The conjunctive floors (above) narrow this compared to the old single blended threshold — a hub can no longer chain purely on date alignment while title similarity is weak, since `needs review` now requires both signals to independently clear 0.65/0.70 — but they don't eliminate the mechanism: a hub whose title genuinely reads as topically adjacent to several unrelated markets (both floors cleared, still not the same event) remains possible in principle and hasn't been independently ruled out against live data the way the deterministic gates were.

  **Correction to an earlier version of this doc**: this was originally written to say the chaining failure "doesn't happen at 0.75," based on the old single-threshold design repeatedly producing 0 groups in testing. That conclusion held only because the dataset available at the time was too sparse and topically skewed to ever trigger it — not because a single number was actually safe from this mechanism. Once fetch coverage improved, the identical transitive-chaining pattern reproduced at the real default in place at the time, no threshold games involved (the numeric-threshold-ladder case immediately above is the same underlying mechanism). That episode is part of why the compensatory single-threshold design was retired in favor of the conjunctive tier floors described above, rather than addressed by picking a different number. Leaving this correction here rather than quietly rewriting the original claim, since a "confirmed conservative" statement turning out to be an artifact of the test data, not the method, is itself worth being able to see.
- The named-entity gate's cost was real enough to become a genuine reliability problem, not just a per-call rounding error — and required two rounds of fixing, not one. First: `extractAllEntities` originally called the extractor once per unique *prefilter-surviving* candidate market, sequentially. Verified live against real fetch-coverage volume (7234 Kalshi markets): a full `equinox match` run took 20+ minutes and was still running when killed, indistinguishable from a hang since nothing was printed in the meantime. **Fixed, first pass**: a bounded worker pool (`entityExtractionConcurrency`, 8 workers) plus a progress callback so a long run is visibly making progress rather than silently stuck. That pass alone wasn't sufficient — verified live a second time, it surfaced a real OpenAI rate limit (500 requests/min for `gpt-4o-mini` on the account used for development) and, once retry-with-backoff and a proactive rate limiter were added to survive that, a *daily* request ceiling (10,000/day) that concurrency and pacing alone can't work around. **Fixed, second pass, the one that actually mattered**: entity extraction is now gated — only run for candidate pairs that already clear the needs-review floor on title similarity and date alignment (both already-computed, free signals), since an entity mismatch can only downgrade an already-qualifying pair, never promote one that title+date alone couldn't reach. Verified live: this cut the number of unique markets needing extraction from 3023 to 37 (~82x) against the same real dataset, with identical final match results, and a full run completed in well under a minute. See docs/DECISIONS.md for the full narrative, including why concurrency and retry/backoff were necessary but not sufficient on their own.
